Part 1

Two decisions before you open Meshy

Bottom line: Rig the gorilla as a humanoid, and generate it from your own words rather than from a picture of someone else's gorilla. Both decisions are hard to reverse once you have spent credits.

Note — what this guide is. It is a supplement, not a numbered chapter. It covers a third-party paid service rather than Godot, so it sits outside the chapter sequence. Chapter 4 assumes you have finished it, but you can substitute any rigged gorilla you like — including the CC-BY Sketchfab model named in Chapter 1 — and skip this entirely.

1. Choose the Humanoid skeleton, not Quadruped

Meshy's web app offers three skeleton types — Humanoid, Quadruped Dog, and Smart Rig (Beta) — and you want Humanoid. Meshy's own tutorial states that humanoid characters have the widest range of ready-made animations and that quadrupeds currently have fewer.

Three reasons Humanoid is right for Uproar specifically:

  1. Your controller already assumes an upright character. The Player capsule is 8 m tall with a 1.5 m radius, and the climbing state lays the character flat against a wall. A knuckle-walking quadruped rig fights all of that.
  2. Godot's retargeting system is humanoid. Godot ships one built-in profile, SkeletonProfileHumanoid. There is no built-in quadruped equivalent.
  3. King Kong stands upright. The reference the game is built on is a biped in almost every shot that matters.
Warning — the Meshy API is more limited than the browser app. Meshy's API documentation states that programmatic rigging currently only works well with standard humanoid bipedal assets, and lists non-humanoid assets among the models auto-rigging is not suitable for. Quadruped and Smart Rig are browser features. Use the web app for everything in this guide.
Note — non-humanoid retargeting is a known weak spot. An open Godot issue reports that animation retargeting for non-humanoid skeletons does not behave correctly, with bones snapping to absolute positions regardless of differing rest poses. [Repo/Issue #103258] This is a second reason to stay on the humanoid path rather than a claim about your specific model.
Expected: you have decided on Humanoid and have not yet spent a credit.

2. Confirm what your plan actually grants you

A paid Meshy plan gives you private ownership of what you generate; the free plan gives you a CC BY 4.0 licence instead. That difference decides whether your credits screen needs an attribution line.

Plan at time of generationWhat you getAttribution
Paid (Pro and above)Full private ownership of the assets you createNone required
FreeCC BY 4.0 licence — commercial use permittedCredit Meshy in your project description

Two conditions attach to the paid grant, both stated in Meshy's own help documentation:

  • You must not publish the asset publicly to the Meshy Community.
  • The source materials, including any uploaded reference image, must not infringe anyone else's copyright.
Warning — do not feed it a King Kong still or the Sketchfab gorilla. Meshy states that uploading a reference you do not own — someone else's artwork, a trademarked character, or licensed IP — can affect your ability to use the resulting model commercially regardless of your plan tier. Generate from a text prompt, or from a photograph or drawing you made yourself. A real gorilla photograph you took is fine; a film frame is not.
Key point — rights are fixed at generation time. Meshy states that models created while you were on a paid plan keep those rights even if you later downgrade. Models generated after a downgrade follow the free plan's CC BY 4.0 terms. If your subscription is about to lapse, generate first.

Action: open your Meshy account settings and confirm the paid plan is active before you generate anything.

Expected: your account shows an active paid plan with credits available.

Source: Meshy Help — ownership of generated models · Meshy Help — commercial use

Part 2

Generate the gorilla

Bottom line: Ask for a T-pose in the prompt itself, judge the mesh before you rig it, and set the polygon budget deliberately rather than accepting the default.

3. Write a prompt that specifies the pose

Put "T-pose" in the prompt text. A T-pose is a character standing with arms straight out sideways, forming a T. An A-pose has the arms angled down at roughly 45 degrees. Meshy's prompt helper suggests pose keywords for exactly this reason.

Key point — why T-pose and not A-pose, even though Meshy prefers A-pose. Meshy states that an A-pose gives more natural shoulder skinning. Godot's retargeting documentation states the opposite preference for its own purposes: with SkeletonProfileHumanoid, the Fix Silhouette import option does not need to be enabled for T-pose models but should be enabled for A-pose models. Choosing T-pose removes an import option you would otherwise have to get right. Slightly worse shoulders is the price, and on a gorilla with fur-shaded shoulders you will not see it.

Action — a starting prompt:

A stylised silverback gorilla, full body, standing upright on two legs,
T-pose, arms straight out to the sides, dark grey fur, broad chest,
game character, clean single mesh

What each part is doing:

  • standing upright on two legs pushes the body plan towards biped, which is what the Humanoid rigger needs to recognise.
  • T-pose, arms straight out to the sides states the pose twice, in the term and in plain words.
  • full body prevents a bust or a head-only result.
  • clean single mesh discourages separate floating parts, which rig badly.
Note — the image path has explicit pose controls. If you use Image to 3D rather than a text prompt, Meshy's tutorial states you can configure the pose before generating, with a T-pose or A-pose toggle and a Custom Pose Control that takes a reference image. Set it there rather than hoping the source image is posed correctly.
Expected: a prompt written down, containing the words "T-pose" and "full body".

Source: Meshy — character auto-rigging workflow · Godot — Retargeting 3D Skeletons

4. Generate, then judge the mesh before rigging it

Rigging quality depends on mesh quality, so inspect the result first. Meshy's tutorial states that very broken geometry or unusual topology may reduce rig quality.

Action:

  1. Open Text to 3D (or Image to 3D) and paste your prompt.
  2. Generate, then rotate the preview and check four things.

The four checks:

  • Arms are separated from the torso. Webbing between an arm and the ribs is the single most common cause of a bad rig, because the skinning cannot tell which bone owns those vertices.
  • Legs are separated from each other. Same reason.
  • It is one mesh, not a body with detached floating pieces.
  • The silhouette reads as a gorilla from the front and the side, not just from the angle the preview opened on.
Warning — if you generated from a photograph, check for background contamination. Meshy's own quick-start documents this failure: a blurry, low-contrast photo with a complex background produces a model that captures background noise with distorted geometry. Their stated fix is to run AI Background Removal first, or to supply a cleaner input image.

If any check fails, regenerate rather than continuing. A rig built on a bad mesh cannot be repaired later without redoing the rig.

Expected: a textured gorilla mesh with clearly separated limbs, saved in your Meshy assets.

Source: Meshy Docs — quick start

5. Set the polygon budget with Remesh

Set the target polycount before export rather than accepting whatever the generator produced. Polycount is the number of triangles in the mesh; more triangles cost more time to draw every frame.

Meshy publishes these typical engine budgets: hero characters around 50,000 triangles, non-player characters around 10,000, and props around 3,000.

Key point — the gorilla is a hero character, but Uproar has exactly one. There is never a second gorilla on screen, so you can afford the upper end. Anywhere between 20,000 and 50,000 triangles is reasonable. The buildings and their fracture shards from Chapters 2 and 3 are where your frame budget actually goes.

Action:

  1. Open Remesh on the generated model.
  2. Set the target polycount within the range above.
  3. Choose triangle topology. Godot renders triangles regardless, so converting later gains you nothing.
Warning — there is a hard face limit on rigging. Meshy's API documentation states that models with more than 300,000 faces are not supported for rigging and must be reduced with Remesh first. A generated character is unlikely to approach that, but if rigging refuses the model, face count is the first thing to check.
Expected: the model reports a polycount in your chosen range.

Source: Meshy Docs — Rigging API · Meshy — game asset budgets

Part 3

Rig it and add motion

Bottom line: Rigging runs in the browser in about thirty seconds. Check the skeleton with the bone overlay before you commit to it, because every animation you add afterwards inherits its mistakes.

6. Run the auto-rigger and inspect the skeleton

Rigging binds a bone hierarchy and skinning weights to the mesh. Skinning weights decide how much each bone pulls on each vertex; they are what makes an elbow bend instead of tearing.

Action:

  1. Open your gorilla in Meshy and choose Rigging.
  2. Select the Humanoid character type, per step 1.
  3. Run the rig. Meshy states this typically completes in under 30 seconds in the browser.
  4. Turn on the bone overlay and rotate the model.

What to look for in the overlay:

  • A spine running up the centre of the torso, not off to one side.
  • Arm chains that end inside the hands, not short of them or past them.
  • Leg chains that reach the feet.
  • No bones outside the mesh.
Key point — you can correct it here rather than in Blender. Meshy states that bones and skinning weights can be fine-tuned in the browser before export. Fixing a misplaced shoulder bone now costs a minute; fixing it after you have applied six animations means redoing them.
Expected: a skeleton overlay that follows the gorilla's limbs, with no bones floating outside the body.

Source: Meshy — AI auto rigging

7. Add the five animations Uproar needs

Add these five clips from the preset library, and no more for now. Meshy states that paid plans unlock over 600 motion presets while free users get more than 20.

ClipUsed byNotes
IdleStanding stillThe default state. Pick a loop with visible breathing.
WalkLow-speed movementMust loop cleanly.
RunFull-speed movementMust loop cleanly.
Attack or punchThe punch in Chapter 1, Phase 4One-shot, not looping.
JumpLeaving the groundOne-shot.
Warning — there is probably no climbing preset, and Uproar needs one. Climbing is the game's signature mechanic and it is not a common preset category. [Unverified] — search the library for "climb" before you assume. If nothing suitable exists, Chapter 4 will cover holding a static climbing pose instead of playing a clip, which is a legitimate solution and cheaper than authoring an animation.
Note — add a sixth clip only if it earns its place. Every clip is another state to wire into the AnimationTree in Chapter 4. Five is enough to build a complete state machine. You can return and add more once the machine works.
Expected: five clips added to the character, each previewing correctly in the Meshy viewer.

8. Note what the bones are called

Meshy claims its rigs use Mixamo's bone-naming conventions. Their auto-rigging page states that bone names follow Mixamo's conventions and that a rigged character can be uploaded to Mixamo and given any motion clip without renaming or a plugin.

Warning — this is a vendor marketing claim, not documentation. [Unverified] It appears on a product page rather than in Meshy's technical docs, and no Godot source confirms it. Treat it as promising rather than settled. Step 11 is where you find out for yourself.

Why it matters to Godot. Godot's retargeting system maps a model's bones onto a SkeletonProfileHumanoid through a BoneMap resource. The engine's own release article states that auto-mapping works when bones carry common English names such as hips, shoulder, arm, leg and foot. Mixamo-style naming fits that description, so if the claim holds, auto-mapping should largely work and Chapter 4 gets considerably shorter.

Key point — if it holds, Mixamo becomes available to you. Chapter 1 correctly states that Mixamo's auto-rigger is bipedal-humanoid only and cannot rig a gorilla. That remains true. But rigging and animating are separate services. A gorilla already rigged by Meshy with Mixamo-compatible naming can potentially be animated by Mixamo, which is a far larger library than the five clips above.
Expected: nothing to do in Meshy. You are noting a claim to test in step 11.

Source: Meshy — AI auto rigging · Godot — Animation retargeting in Godot 4.0

Part 4

Export and file it

Bottom line: Download both GLB and FBX, because the two sources disagree about which is safer, and one extra download costs nothing.

9. Download the rigged, animated character

Export as GLB first, then repeat the export as FBX. Meshy's tutorial gives the path as Download → Animation → All Added → Single File, offering FBX, GLB, or USDZ.

Key point — the two sources disagree, so take both formats. Godot's documentation lists glTF 2.0, which .glb is the binary form of, as the recommended import format. Meshy states that FBX is the most reliable choice for preserving skeletons and skinning across game engines. Both are credible. Download GLB for the project and keep the FBX beside it as a fallback for when a bone or a weight looks wrong.

Action:

  1. Choose Download, then Animation, then All Added, then Single File.
  2. Select GLB and download.
  3. Repeat, selecting FBX.
  4. Put both files somewhere outside your Godot project for now.
Warning — download rather than relying on the cloud copy. Meshy's API documentation shows an expires_at timestamp on task results, marking when output assets expire and may be deleted. Whether the browser app applies the same retention is not documented, so keep your own copy.
Note — "Single File" matters. It packages the mesh, the skeleton, and every animation clip into one file. The alternative gives you separate files per clip, which Godot can use through its As AnimationLibrary import mode — but that is the harder path and Chapter 4 does not need it.
Expected: two files on disk, one .glb and one .fbx, both larger than the unrigged model.

Source: Meshy — character auto-rigging workflow · Meshy Docs — Rigging API

10. Rename and place the file

Rename the GLB to gorilla.glb before it enters the project. Meshy exports with a generated filename such as Character_output.glb, which tells you nothing in six months.

Action:

  1. Rename the GLB to gorilla.glb and the FBX to gorilla-backup.fbx.
  2. In Godot, open the FileSystem dock and select res://asset/model/.
  3. Drag gorilla.glb into that folder. Godot imports it and writes a matching .import file beside it.
  4. Keep gorilla-backup.fbx outside the project, so Godot does not import a second copy of the same character.
Warning — move files inside Godot, never in your operating system's file manager. This is the same rule as Chapter 1, Phase 0. Dragging a file within the FileSystem dock makes Godot rewrite every reference to it; moving it outside Godot breaks those references silently.
Expected: res://asset/model/ contains gorilla.glb, and the FileSystem dock shows it with a scene icon rather than an error.

Part 5

Verify it in Godot

Bottom line: Two checks decide whether Chapter 4 is short or long — whether the animations arrived, and what the bones are called. Do both before starting Chapter 4.

11. Open the model and read the bone list

This is the test that settles the naming question from step 8. An imported 3D scene is read-only in Godot, so you open an inherited scene to inspect it.

Action:

  1. In the FileSystem dock, right-click gorilla.glb.
  2. Choose New Inherited Scene. Godot opens a scene tree built from the file.
  3. Find the Skeleton3D node and the AnimationPlayer node in the tree.
  4. Select the AnimationPlayer and open its animation dropdown. Count the clips.
  5. Select the Skeleton3D and read the bone names listed in the Inspector.
  6. Write down the first six bone names exactly as they appear.

What the bone names tell you:

  • Names like mixamorig:Hips, Hips, Spine, LeftArm confirm the Mixamo convention. Godot's auto-mapping should then largely work, and Chapter 4 can lean on it.
  • Names like bone_01, joint_3, or anything numeric mean auto-mapping will fail and every bone must be assigned by hand in the BoneMap.
Warning — do not enable retargeting yet. Godot's retargeting documentation warns that the rest-fixing option, which overwrites Bone Rests to match the profile's reference pose, can produce very poor results when the original Bone Rest matters. Chapter 4 will decide whether you need it at all. Just look at the names for now.
Expected: a scene containing a Skeleton3D and an AnimationPlayer, the AnimationPlayer listing five clips, and six bone names written down.

Source: Godot — Retargeting 3D Skeletons

12. Check the scale and the facing direction

Meshy builds characters at human scale; your gorilla needs to be roughly 8 metres tall. The Player capsule is a CapsuleShape3D with radius 1.5 and height 8, positioned at y 4, so its top sits 8 metres above the floor.

Action:

  1. Open res://scene/player.tscn.
  2. Drag gorilla.glb from the FileSystem dock onto the Facing node — not onto Player, and not onto CameraPivot.
  3. Adjust the imported node's Scale until the gorilla's head reaches the top of the capsule.
  4. If the gorilla faces away from you, set its Rotation on Y to 180.
Key point — the model goes under Facing, and the capsule stays. Facing is the only node that rotates, so the visual model must live under it to turn with the character. The CollisionShape3D remains on Player and remains the capsule — physics never uses the detailed mesh, which is both correct and far faster.
Note — why 180 degrees. Godot treats -Z as forward for a Node3D. Many exported models are authored facing +Z. Meshy's API documentation confirms this expectation from the other direction: it requires uploaded models to face +Z, which it calls the standard glTF forward direction.

Delete the placeholder. The old Body MeshInstance3D under Facing can go once the gorilla is in place. Rename the imported node to Body so the existing script paths still resolve.

Expected: pressing F6 on player.tscn shows a gorilla roughly filling the capsule, facing away from the camera, moving and punching exactly as the capsule did.

Source: Meshy Docs — Rigging API, forward axis

Key point — what to carry into Chapter 4. Three things: the six bone names from step 11, the number of animation clips the AnimationPlayer found, and whether a climbing clip exists. Chapter 4 builds an AnimationTree on top of all three.
